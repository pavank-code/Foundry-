- A single issue has been reported repeatedly.

- Incident priority: which individual issue needs attention first?

1. Priority ranking: what matters most.

2. Mathematical approach I — Bayesian risk estimation
Purpose: Estimate how much attention a location deserves while explicitly representing uncertainty.
A raw incident count is insufficient. A location with two severe incidents may deserve more attention than a location with ten minor complaints. Meanwhile, locations with little reporting may have artificially low counts.
2.1 Estimate an underlying incident rate
Let:
- \(i\) represent a location.
- \(t\) represent a time window.
- \(N_{i,t}\) represent the number of accepted incidents.
- \(E_{i,t}\) represent exposure, such as pedestrian activity or traffic volume, when reliably available.
- \(\lambda_{i,t}\) represent the underlying incident rate.
A basic count model is:
\[
N_{i,t}\sim\operatorname{Poisson}(E_{i,t}\lambda_{i,t})
\]

The Poisson assumption is a starting point, not a universal truth. If incidents cluster or the variance exceeds the mean, a negative-binomial model or point process may fit better.
A Bayesian prior can represent the uncertainty in the rate:
\[
\lambda_i\sim\operatorname{Gamma}(\alpha_0,\beta_0)
\]

Using the Gamma distribution's shape-rate parameterization, after observing \(N_i\) incidents over exposure \(E_i\):
\[
\lambda_i\mid N_i,E_i
\sim
\operatorname{Gamma}(\alpha_0+N_i,\beta_0+E_i)
\]

The posterior mean is:
\[
\mathbb E[\lambda_i\mid N_i,E_i]
=
\frac{\alpha_0+N_i}{\beta_0+E_i}
\]

This prevents us from interpreting a tiny number of observations as a perfectly reliable risk estimate. Prior parameters should be estimated from suitable training data or documented assumptions.
Why this is useful
The system can display both the estimated rate and an uncertainty interval. A location with a high estimated rate but very little evidence can be marked differently from a location with a similarly high rate supported by many observations.
Data requirement: exposure data should eventually include traffic volume or pedestrian counts. Population density is only a proxy and should not automatically be treated as a direct measure of road-user exposure.
2.2 Estimate severity-weighted expected harm
Rather than simply counting incidents, estimate the probability of each severity outcome.
Let \(S\) be the severity category and \(C(S)\) be a documented consequence weight. Then:
\[
H_i =
\mathbb E\left[
\sum_{k=1}^{N_i} C(S_k)
\mid \mathcal D
\right]
\]

Here, \(\mathcal D\) is the available evidence for location \(i\).
For example, the model might estimate expected severity-weighted incident burden over the next 7 days or 30 days. The consequence weights should be agreed with the domain owner and tested for sensitivity, rather than invented by the model.
The final score can combine expected harm with urgency, exposure, backlog and intervention feasibility—but those dimensions should remain separately visible.
2.3 Add uncertainty to the decision
A risk estimate should not become more confident merely because the interface displays a precise decimal.
Possible outputs include:
- Posterior mean expected incident rate.
- 90% or 95% credible interval.
- Probability that the location exceeds an approved risk threshold.
- Evidence count and freshness.
- Sensitivity to prior assumptions.
For example:
\[
P(\lambda_i>\lambda_{\text{critical}}\mid\mathcal D)
\]

is the probability that a location's underlying incident rate exceeds a defined threshold given the observed evidence.
This is more interpretable than presenting a score of 87.42 with no uncertainty information.

3. Mathematical approach II — Spatiotemporal forecasting
Purpose: Detect emerging hotspots and estimate whether incident activity is changing over time.
This is where point processes, time-series models and spatial models become valuable.
3.1 Hawkes processes for incident clustering
A Hawkes process models events whose occurrence can temporarily increase the probability of further events.
Let \(\lambda_i(t)\) be the instantaneous incident intensity at location \(i\):
\[
\lambda_i(t)
=
\mu_i(t)
+
\sum_{t_k<t}
\alpha_{ik}g(t-t_k)
\]

where:
- \(\mu_i(t)\) is the baseline incident intensity.
- \(t_k\) are previous incident times.
- \(\alpha_{ik}\) represents the triggering contribution of a previous event.
- \(g(\cdot)\) is a decay kernel.
A common exponential kernel is:
\[
g(\tau)=\beta e^{-\beta\tau},\qquad \tau>0
\]

This captures the idea that recent incidents can carry more predictive information than incidents from months ago.
For example, a burst of pothole complaints or road incidents may indicate a worsening underlying road condition. But this is a statistical association, not proof that one report caused another.
Spatial extension
We can connect nearby locations using a spatial interaction kernel:
\[
\lambda_i(t)
=
\mu_i(t)
+
\sum_{k:t_k<t}
\alpha
K_s(d_{ik})
K_t(t-t_k)
\]

where \(d_{ik}\) is the geographic distance between locations, \(K_s\) is a spatial kernel, and \(K_t\) is a temporal decay kernel.
This helps the system detect clusters across neighboring locations rather than treating every location as isolated.
Important limitation: the current data contains timestamps and simulated coordinates, but it does not establish a reliable causal propagation mechanism. A Hawkes model should be compared against simpler baselines and only retained if it improves out-of-sample forecasts.
3.2 Markov models for changing risk regimes
A city does not necessarily behave the same way every week. Incident activity may move between low, normal, elevated or unusually high states.
Let the hidden state at time \(t\) be \(Z_t\). A Markov model assumes:
\[
P(Z_t\mid Z_{t-1},Z_{t-2},\ldots)
=
P(Z_t\mid Z_{t-1})
\]

The transition probabilities are represented by:
\[
A_{ab}=P(Z_t=b\mid Z_{t-1}=a)
\]

For a hidden Markov model, observed features \(X_t\), such as incident counts, severity mix and complaint backlog, are generated conditionally on the hidden state:
\[
P(X_{1:T},Z_{1:T})
=
P(Z_1)
\prod_{t=2}^{T}P(Z_t\mid Z_{t-1})
\prod_{t=1}^{T}P(X_t\mid Z_t)
\]

The model can estimate:
\[
P(Z_t=\text{elevated}\mid X_{1:t})
\]

This can trigger an alert when the probability of an elevated regime rises sufficiently.
Where it fits in the product
The regime detector could show:
- Locations moving from normal to elevated activity.
- Zones experiencing sustained increases.
- A high-priority location whose incident frequency is accelerating.
- Whether a spike appears temporary or persistent.
A Markov model does not inherently predict exact incident counts. Pair it with a count-forecasting model if numerical forecasts are required.
How we would train it
- Aggregate observations into consistent hourly, daily or weekly windows.
- Use only information available up to each forecast time.
- Fit the transition and emission parameters.
- Select the number of states using held-out likelihood, information criteria and interpretability.
- Test whether inferred states are stable across time periods.
We should not label hidden states as “dangerous” merely because the algorithm assigns them state number 2. Their meanings must be established from their observed characteristics and validated operationally.
3.3 Forecast counts with negative-binomial regression
Incident counts often show overdispersion, where the variance is greater than the mean. A negative-binomial model can account for this:
\[
N_{i,t}\sim\operatorname{NegBin}(\mu_{i,t},\phi)
\]

\[
\log \mu_{i,t}
=
\log E_{i,t}
+
\beta^\top x_{i,t}
+
u_i
+
v_t
\]

Here:
- \(x_{i,t}\) includes lagged incident counts, road condition and other available predictors.
- \(u_i\) captures location-specific effects.
- \(v_t\) captures temporal effects.
- \(\log E_{i,t}\) is an exposure offset when a suitable exposure measure exists.
Compare this with a Poisson model using held-out predictive likelihood, calibration and forecast error.
The model should output expected counts and prediction intervals, not just a point forecast.

4. Mathematical approach III — Spatial graph intelligence
Purpose: Understand relationships between locations, detect spatial clusters and estimate how neighboring evidence should affect a location's risk estimate.
The city can be represented as a graph:
\[
G=(V,E)
\]

where each node \(i\in V\) represents a location and each edge \((i,j)\in E\) represents a meaningful spatial or road-network relationship.
The graph can be constructed from:
- Geographic proximity.
- Road connectivity, when available.
- Shared intersections or road segments.
- Similarity of validated infrastructure characteristics.
Geographic proximity alone is not enough to establish road connectivity.
4.1 Spatial smoothing
A conditional autoregressive-style model can account for spatial dependence:
\[
\eta_i=x_i^\top\beta+u_i
\]

where \(u_i\) is a location-specific spatial effect. Neighboring locations can be encouraged to have related effects through a graph-based prior:
\[
p(u\mid G)
\propto
\exp\left(
-\frac{\tau}{2}
\sum_{(i,j)\in E}w_{ij}(u_i-u_j)^2
\right)
\]

Here, \(w_{ij}\) measures the strength of the connection and \(\tau\) controls the amount of smoothing.
This helps stabilize estimates when data is sparse, while allowing disconnected or distinctly different locations to behave differently.
The model should not smooth away real hotspots. We would test it against a model without spatial smoothing and evaluate whether it improves predictions on unseen periods and locations.
4.2 Graph neural networks — later, if justified
A graph neural network can combine each location's features with features from its neighbors:
\[
h_i^{(\ell+1)}
=
\sigma\left(
W_{\text{self}}h_i^{(\ell)}
+
W_{\text{neighbor}}
\operatorname{AGG}
\{h_j^{(\ell)}:j\in\mathcal N(i)\}
\right)
\]

Here, \(h_i^{(\ell)}\) is the representation of location \(i\) at layer \(\ell\), and \(\operatorname{AGG}\) aggregates neighboring representations.
Potential uses:
- Forecasting risk across connected road segments.
- Detecting clusters that cross administrative zone boundaries.
- Learning interactions between location condition and neighboring incident patterns.
However, a graph neural network is not automatically better than a graph-based statistical model. With 1,000 locations and synthetic data, it may overfit. It belongs in the experimental track until we have enough independent, reliable observations.

5. Mathematical approach IV — Predicting resolution time with survival analysis
Purpose: Estimate how long a complaint or incident is likely to remain unresolved.
This is important because a repair that has remained unresolved for 20 days may require a different operational response from a new report, even if their initial priority scores are similar.
Survival analysis is especially suitable because some records may still be unresolved at the end of observation. We cannot treat those cases as if their final resolution time were known.
5.1 The survival function
Let \(T\) be the time until resolution.
The survival function is:
\[
S(t\mid x)=P(T>t\mid x)
\]

It measures the probability that the issue remains unresolved beyond time \(t\), given features \(x\).
The hazard function is:
\[
h(t\mid x)
=
\lim_{\Delta t\to0}
\frac{
P(t\leq T<t+\Delta t\mid T\geq t,x)
}{\Delta t}
\]

It represents the instantaneous resolution rate among issues that are still unresolved.
5.2 Cox proportional hazards model
A classical starting point is:
\[
h(t\mid x)
=
h_0(t)\exp(\beta^\top x)
\]

where \(h_0(t)\) is the baseline hazard and \(x\) can contain incident type, severity, zone, complaint category and other features known at the relevant prediction time.
A model with a larger hazard predicts faster resolution, all else equal. It does not imply lower incident risk or lower severity.
Product features enabled
- Predicted probability of remaining unresolved after 7 or 14 days.
- Expected resolution-time distribution.
- Alerts for complaints likely to become long-running cases.
- Identification of categories or zones with persistent backlogs.
- Resource planning based on predicted workload duration.
The existing files contain resolution durations and status fields, but we must inspect how unresolved records are represented. If unresolved records do not have a valid observation cutoff or censoring information, we cannot correctly train a standard survival model on them without additional assumptions.
5.3 Evaluation
Use:
- Concordance index for ranking resolution times.
- Integrated Brier score for probabilistic predictions.
- Calibration of predicted probabilities of remaining unresolved.
- Time-based validation to avoid training on future information.
This gives the resource planner a forecast of how much work may remain open—not merely a prediction of whether an issue will be resolved.

6. Mathematical approach V — Gradient-based machine learning
Purpose: Learn nonlinear relationships between civic conditions and future incident outcomes.
This is where gradient descent, loss functions, embeddings and more advanced prediction models come into play.
I would not make a deep neural network the first predictive model. Start with strong statistical baselines and gradient-boosted trees, then test neural approaches against them.
6.1 Supervised prediction target
Define a future outcome clearly. For example:
\[
Y_{i,t}^{(7)}
=
\mathbf 1[
\text{at least one qualifying incident at location }i
\text{ during }(t,t+7\text{ days}]
]
\]

The feature vector \(x_{i,t}\) must only contain information available at time \(t\).
A classifier estimates:
\[
p_{i,t}
=
P(Y_{i,t}^{(7)}=1\mid x_{i,t})
\]

This probability could help forecast the chance of a qualifying incident over the next seven days. It is not the same thing as incident severity, expected harm or repair urgency.
6.2 Logistic regression and gradient descent
For a transparent baseline:
\[
p_i=\sigma(w^\top x_i+b)
\]

where:
\[
\sigma(z)=\frac{1}{1+e^{-z}}
\]

The binary cross-entropy loss is:
\[
\mathcal L(w,b)
=
-\frac1N\sum_{i=1}^{N}
\left[
y_i\log p_i+(1-y_i)\log(1-p_i)
\right]
+
\lambda\lVert w\rVert_2^2
\]

Gradient descent updates the parameters:
\[
\theta_{k+1}
=
\theta_k-\eta\nabla_\theta\mathcal L(\theta_k)
\]

where \(\theta=(w,b)\), \(\eta\) is the learning rate, and the regularization term discourages unnecessarily large coefficients.
This is real parameter learning: the model estimates weights from historical outcomes rather than relying on manually chosen score weights.
However, logistic regression only provides a useful prediction if the target is meaningful, the features are valid and the training/evaluation split reflects the intended deployment setting.
6.3 Gradient-boosted trees
For tabular civic data, LightGBM, XGBoost or CatBoost are strong candidates to benchmark.
A boosted model has the form:
\[
F_M(x)=F_0(x)+\sum_{m=1}^{M}\nu f_m(x)
\]

Each new tree \(f_m\) is trained to improve the current model's loss, with \(\nu\) controlling the learning rate.
Advantages:
- Learns nonlinear interactions.
- Handles mixed numeric and categorical features.
- Can model effects such as road condition interacting with incident recurrence.
- Usually needs less data than a large neural network.
- Can provide feature attributions for explanation, although attribution is not proof of causality.
Use logistic regression and a suitable count model as baselines. Keep a boosted model only if it improves out-of-time predictive performance and remains adequately calibrated.
6.4 A multi-task model
Longer-term, a shared model could learn representations for related outcomes:
\[
h_i=f_\theta(x_i)
\]

\[
\begin{aligned}
\hat y_i^{\text{incident}}&=g_1(h_i)\\
\hat y_i^{\text{severity}}&=g_2(h_i)\\
\hat y_i^{\text{resolution}}&=g_3(h_i)
\end{aligned}
\]

The joint objective might be:
\[
\mathcal L_{\text{total}}
=
\lambda_1\mathcal L_{\text{incident}}
+
\lambda_2\mathcal L_{\text{severity}}
+
\lambda_3\mathcal L_{\text{resolution}}
\]

This lets related tasks share statistical information. It should only be attempted when there are sufficient valid labels for each target, and when the tasks truly benefit from shared representations.
Critical limitation: the supplied dataset is not yet enough to train and validate every future-outcome model rigorously. We have historical records, but the product needs explicit prediction cutoffs, future target windows and leakage-safe splits before we can claim forecasting ability.

7. Mathematical approach VI — Embeddings for unfamiliar civic data
Purpose: Let the ingestion system recognize unfamiliar schemas, categories and text without hard-coding every possible column name.
This directly supports our first-stage data-source dashboard.
We should use different kinds of embeddings for different problems. A text embedding is not a substitute for numeric validation or location identity.
7.1 Semantic schema mapping
Suppose a new source contains columns named:
- site_ref
- hazard_class
- reported_on
- repair_elapsed
Our canonical schema expects fields such as location_id, severity, timestamp and resolution_hours.
For each source field, construct a representation using:
\[
e_j=f_{\text{embed}}(
\text{name}_j,
\text{description}_j,
\text{sample values}_j,
\text{source context}
)
\]

Compare it with embeddings for canonical field descriptions \(c_k\):
\[
s_{jk}
=
\frac{e_j^\top c_k}
{\lVert e_j\rVert\lVert c_k\rVert}
\]

The cosine similarity \(s_{jk}\) provides a candidate-match score.
But similarity alone is not enough. We should combine it with type compatibility, value patterns, required-field rules and domain constraints:
\[
M_{jk}
=
a s_{jk}
+b\,T_{jk}
+c\,V_{jk}
+d\,D_{jk}
\]

where:
- \(T_{jk}\): type compatibility.
- \(V_{jk}\): sample-value compatibility.
- \(D_{jk}\): domain-rule compatibility.
- \(a,b,c,d\): validated weights.
A constrained assignment algorithm can then propose mappings while enforcing rules such as one source field not being assigned to two incompatible canonical fields.
Novel capability: confidence-aware mapping
The system should estimate whether a mapping is safe to apply automatically.
For example:
- A strong, validated match to location_id: auto-map.
- Similarity between priority and multiple possible fields: request confirmation.
- No credible match: preserve as unknown.
A calibrated mapping model can estimate the probability that a proposed mapping is correct, based on a labelled set of reviewed mappings.
The decision threshold should be selected according to the cost of mistakes. Misidentifying a column that changes incident severity is more consequential than failing to map an optional descriptive note.
7.2 Semantic complaint clustering
Embeddings can also group complaint descriptions that use different words for similar problems.
For text complaint \(j\):
\[
z_j=f_{\text{embed}}(\text{text}_j)
\]

Then compare complaints using cosine similarity, or cluster their vectors using an appropriate algorithm.
Potential applications:
- Grouping synonymous complaint categories.
- Identifying recurring descriptions of the same infrastructure problem.
- Surfacing emerging categories not present in the original schema.
- Suggesting duplicate reports for human review.
However, semantic similarity is not proof that two reports describe the same incident. Duplicate detection should combine semantic similarity with location, time, source identity and other available evidence.
The current complaint dataset has structured categories, not necessarily rich free-text descriptions. This feature needs new text inputs or a later data source to become useful.
7.3 Entity resolution
Embeddings can help propose matches between inconsistent location names or descriptions. For structured location IDs, exact matching and approved crosswalks remain preferable.
A candidate-match score could be:
\[
R(a,b)
=
\alpha s_{\text{text}}(a,b)
+
\beta s_{\text{geo}}(a,b)
+
\gamma s_{\text{attributes}}(a,b)
\]

The system should use a review threshold and preserve the evidence supporting each match. A false match between two different road segments can corrupt every downstream ranking.

8. Mathematical approach VII — Resource allocation as constrained optimization
Purpose: Turn a priority ranking into a feasible action plan under real operational constraints.
This is one of the strongest mathematical parts of the project because it combines predictive analytics with operations research.
8.1 Decision variables
Let:
- \(i\) index candidate interventions.
- \(r\) index available crews or resources.
- \(x_{ir}\in\{0,1\}\) indicate whether resource \(r\) is assigned to intervention \(i\).
- \(b_i\) be the estimated benefit of intervention \(i\).
- \(c_i\) be its cost.
- \(t_{ir}\) be the time required from resource \(r\).
- \(B\) be the available budget.
- \(H_r\) be the available capacity of resource \(r\).
The benefit \(b_i\) should ideally represent expected reduction in harm or backlog, not simply the original priority score.
A basic optimization model is:
\[
\max_{x}\sum_i\sum_r b_i x_{ir}
\]

subject to:
\[
\sum_i c_i x_{ir}\leq B
\]

and resource-capacity constraints such as:
\[
\sum_i t_{ir}x_{ir}\leq H_r
\]

plus assignment and eligibility constraints. If each intervention requires a specific skill, only eligible crews should be allowed to perform it.
This is a simplified illustration. The actual formulation needs to account for whether costs are incurred per intervention or per assignment, multi-crew jobs, scheduling conflicts and dependencies.
8.2 Multiple objectives
A city may care about several outcomes at once:
- Reduce expected harm.
- Reduce the unresolved complaint backlog.
- Protect vulnerable road users.
- Reduce geographic service imbalances.
- Avoid wasting scarce resources on low-impact interventions.
A weighted objective could be:
\[
\max_x
\left(
w_H H(x)
+w_B B_{\text{reduced}}(x)
+w_E E_{\text{equity}}(x)
-w_C C(x)
\right)
\]

But there is a catch: arbitrary weights can hide important trade-offs. For public safety, some requirements may be better represented as hard constraints or minimum service guarantees rather than soft preferences.
We should also provide Pareto comparisons, allowing decision-makers to see alternatives that trade off cost, expected benefit and coverage.
8.3 The uncertainty-aware version
The predicted benefit of an intervention is uncertain. Represent it as a random variable \(B_i\), rather than pretending the expected benefit is known exactly.
A risk-sensitive objective might be:
\[
\max_x
\left[
\mathbb E[U(x)]
-\kappa\operatorname{Risk}(U(x))
\right]
\]

where \(U(x)\) is the total intervention utility and \(\kappa\) controls risk aversion.
For a more rigorous formulation, we can optimize a lower confidence bound or impose a chance constraint such as:
\[
P(\text{required coverage is achieved})\geq 1-\epsilon
\]

The correct choice depends on the decision and available evidence. We should not use uncertainty adjustments as a way to systematically penalize locations with sparse reporting.
What the frontend would show
The resource planner should display:
- The selected interventions and assigned crews.
- Budget and capacity usage.
- Expected benefit with uncertainty.
- Uncovered high-priority locations.
- Reasons an intervention was excluded.
- The difference between the current allocation and the proposed allocation.
- Alternative plans when constraints change.
This is the mathematical basis of the Resource-Aware Action Engine.

9. Mathematical approach VIII — Causal inference and intervention evaluation
This is essential if we want to claim that the platform actually improves outcomes, rather than simply predicting them.
A predictive model estimates what may happen. A causal model attempts to estimate what changes because of a specific intervention.
Let:
- \(Y_i(1)\) be the outcome for location \(i\) if an intervention is applied.
- \(Y_i(0)\) be the outcome if it is not applied.
The treatment effect is:
\[
\tau_i=\mathbb E[Y_i(1)-Y_i(0)\mid X_i]
\]

For a repair, the outcome could be the subsequent rate of a relevant incident type or time to resolution. The sign convention should be chosen so that improvement is clearly defined.
The central difficulty is that we cannot observe both outcomes for the same location at the same time. A location chosen for repair may already be more dangerous than a location that was not repaired.
This is confounding by indication: high-risk locations are more likely to receive interventions.
Candidate methods
- Difference-in-differences: compare changes over time between treated and comparable untreated locations, subject to assumptions such as parallel trends.
- Propensity-score weighting: adjust for observed differences in the likelihood of receiving an intervention.
- Doubly robust estimation: combine treatment and outcome models, under the required identification assumptions.
- Randomized or phased rollouts: where ethically and operationally appropriate, provide stronger evidence for intervention effects.
A simple difference-in-differences model might be:
\[
Y_{it}
=
\alpha_i+\gamma_t
+\delta(\text{Treated}_i\times\text{Post}_t)
+\epsilon_{it}
\]

The coefficient \(\delta\) estimates the intervention-associated change under the model's assumptions.
Product application
The Decision Receipt can record:
- Why the location was selected.
- What intervention was approved.
- When the intervention occurred.
- What outcome was observed afterward.
- Whether the evaluation method supports a causal conclusion or only an association.
The existing synthetic files do not establish a reliable history of intervention assignments and outcomes. We need those records before the product can credibly learn which repairs work best.
We should never claim causal improvement merely because incidents declined after a repair. Weather, seasonality, reporting behavior, regression to the mean and other changes may explain the decline.

10. Mathematical approach IX — Calibration, fairness and decision confidence
A model can have good accuracy and still produce poor decisions if its probabilities are systematically wrong or its rankings are unstable.
10.1 Probability calibration
Suppose the model predicts a 70% probability of an incident for 100 comparable location-windows. If those predictions are well calibrated, approximately 70 should experience the defined event, over a sufficiently large and representative evaluation sample.
Use:
- Reliability diagrams.
- Brier score.
- Log loss.
- Calibration error, with appropriate sample-size caveats.
The Brier score for a binary target is:
\[
\operatorname{BS}
=
\frac1N\sum_{i=1}^{N}(p_i-y_i)^2
\]

Lower is better. It evaluates probabilistic accuracy, not the quality of every downstream decision.
Calibration methods such as isotonic regression or Platt scaling can be fitted on a separate calibration set. They should not be fitted on the final test set.
10.2 Ranking quality
Because the product ranks locations, probability calibration is not sufficient.
Evaluate:
- Precision@\(k\): how many of the top \(k\) locations contain the target outcome?
- Recall@\(k\): how many qualifying outcomes are captured by the top \(k\)?
- NDCG@\(k\): how effectively the ranking places more relevant outcomes near the top.
- Cost-weighted recall: whether the queue captures the most consequential outcomes.
- Ranking stability: how much the queue changes when small input changes occur.
For example:
\[
\operatorname{Precision@}k
=
\frac{\text{qualifying cases among top }k}{k}
\]

The definition of a “qualifying case” must be specified before evaluation. We should not simply treat the historical ranking as ground truth, because the model may reproduce past reporting or allocation biases.
10.3 Fairness and service coverage
Fairness should be evaluated in terms of outcomes and service access, not only equal score distributions.
Possible measures include:
\[
\operatorname{Coverage}_z
=
\frac{\text{eligible high-priority needs served in zone }z}
{\text{eligible high-priority needs in zone }z}
\]

Compare coverage, response time, unresolved backlog and false-negative rates across zones, while accounting for legitimate differences in need and constraints.
The optimizer may impose minimum coverage or maximum service-gap constraints if the municipality approves them. Those policies should be visible, auditable and tested for unintended consequences.
10.4 Confidence-aware prioritization
I recommend showing three separate indicators for every location:

Priority
How urgent is the estimated need?



Evidence confidence
How reliable is the estimate given data volume, freshness and model uncertainty?



Actionability
Can the team act now, given resources, dependencies and operational constraints?



This is better than collapsing urgency, confidence and feasibility into one opaque number.

11. Mathematical approach X — LLMs as semantic agents, not the predictive core
We absolutely can use LLMs, embeddings and agentic workflows. But the LLM should not be responsible for inventing risk probabilities or allocating resources without a verified calculation.
I would give the LLM five bounded roles.
Role	Mathematical or technical backbone	LLM responsibility
Schema assistant	Embedding similarity, type checks, constrained field matching	Explain proposed mappings and request confirmation
Incident summarizer	Structured records and approved evidence retrieval	Summarize what is known and cite supporting records
Investigation assistant	Search, filters and deterministic analytical tools	Formulate queries and explain results
Decision copilot	Forecasts and constrained optimizer	Compare approved alternatives and communicate trade-offs
Review assistant	Validation rules and provenance	Explain errors and suggest corrections
Tool-mediated reasoning
The architecture should be:
\[
\text{Question}
\rightarrow
\text{Authorized query}
\rightarrow
\text{Validated computation}
\rightarrow
\text{Evidence retrieval}
\rightarrow
\text{Explanation}
\]

For a question such as “Why did this location rise in priority?”, the assistant should call tools that retrieve the previous and current ranking versions, their contributing records and the scoring configuration. It should not answer from language-model memory.
Guardrails
- Every quantitative claim links to retrieved evidence or a reproducible computation.
- Unknown data remains unknown.
- Proposed mappings require review when confidence is inadequate.
- Resource assignments require authorization.
- The LLM cannot bypass validation, access controls or audit logging.
- The final numerical score comes from the validated scoring service, not from generated text.
This makes the LLM an interface to the analytical system rather than a substitute for it.

12. How we validate the entire system
The most important slide in a serious ML presentation may be the one explaining how we prevent the model from fooling ourselves.
12.1 Temporal validation
Never randomly split rows from a time-dependent dataset if that allows future information to leak into training.
Use a chronological design:
- Train on an earlier period.
- Tune on a subsequent period.
- Test on a later, untouched period.
- Repeat with rolling-origin backtests where enough history exists.
Every feature must be computed from information available at its prediction cutoff.
For example, using a complaint's final days_to_resolution to predict its initial priority would leak the future outcome.
12.2 Baseline comparisons
Every advanced method must beat an appropriate simpler baseline.
Advanced method	Baseline
Bayesian incident-rate model	Historical rate or simple count model
Hawkes process	Rolling average and Poisson/negative-binomial forecast
Hidden Markov regime model	Fixed thresholds and rolling statistics
Graph model	Independent location model
Gradient-boosted prediction	Logistic regression or generalized linear model
Survival model	Kaplan–Meier curves or a simple baseline hazard
Embedding-based mapping	Approved aliases and deterministic mapping rules
Resource optimizer	Current allocation and priority-greedy baseline
If the advanced model fails to improve the right metric, we should not deploy it just because it sounds more sophisticated.
12.3 Operational acceptance criteria
The system must also demonstrate that:
- A new event is ingested without reloading all historical data.
- The affected aggregates update correctly.
- A correction does not double-count an event.
- A ranking change can be explained from source records.
- Resource constraints are respected.
- An optimizer's output can be reproduced from its inputs and version.
- A missing or unreliable input cannot silently become a confident prediction.
- A failed data pipeline does not publish a misleading new ranking.
The numeric thresholds for forecast quality, mapping accuracy and update latency should be agreed after we establish a baseline and intended operational requirements. We should not invent impressive-looking performance numbers for the presentation.

14. What we should implement first
Not every mathematical method should be active on day one. I would use this staged research plan.
Stage	Build and validate	Reason
Foundation	Data validation, canonical schema, deterministic scoring, Bayesian rate estimation	Establish trustworthy inputs and a defensible baseline.
Prediction	Count forecasting, gradient-boosted models, resolution-time survival model	Learn whether historical data provides predictive signal.
Dynamic intelligence	Markov regimes, hotspot models, spatial dependence	Add temporal and geographic intelligence if baselines justify it.
Decision optimization	Constrained allocation, scenario analysis, sensitivity analysis	Convert evidence into feasible intervention plans.

The data source dashboard and auditability layer remain foundational throughout every stage.
Final technical thesis
The system should not be described as one giant AI model. It should be described as a modular mathematical decision system where each component has a specific responsibility:
- Bayesian inference estimates risk and uncertainty.
- Temporal models forecast changes in incident activity.
- Spatial models account for relationships between locations.
- Survival analysis estimates resolution times.
- Gradient-based learning predicts defined future outcomes.
- Embeddings help interpret heterogeneous inputs.
- Constrained optimization selects feasible interventions.
- Causal inference evaluates actual intervention effects.
- LLMs help users interact with the system and understand its evidence.
That combination is both mathematically substantive and operationally coherent.
The biggest missing ingredient is not another algorithm. It is reliable longitudinal data about actual interventions, resources, and subsequent outcomes. The supplied files are enough to prototype and compare some initial models, but that additional data is what will eventually let the system move from describing historical problems to forecasting outcomes and learning which actions work.
